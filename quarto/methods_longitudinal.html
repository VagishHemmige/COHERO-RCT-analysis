This page explains the model behind the repeated-measures outcomes (ACT, AQLQ, PACQLQ, AFS and self-reported adherence) in general terms. It says nothing about this trial’s results. The last methods page shows where each idea appears in the code.

Why repeated measures need their own model

In a trial with visits at baseline and at several follow-up times, each participant contributes several measurements. Measurements from the same person are more alike than measurements from different people: someone with well-controlled asthma at baseline tends to stay better controlled than average. Treating all rows as independent ignores that relationship and can give incorrect uncertainty estimates; the direction of the error depends on the comparison.

A linear mixed model represents this with a participant-specific shift in the expected score, a random intercept. The fixed effects describe the average course of each arm over time, and the random intercept absorbs each person’s persistent difference from that average. This is the model fitted with lme4 (Bates et al. 2015):

Click to show/hide R Code 
# Each participant (study_id_screening) gets a random intercept
lmer(act_score ~ time_factor * studyarm + (1 | study_id_screening), data = visits)

In this model, a random intercept together with constant residual variance assumes equal residual correlation between any two visits from the same person, however far apart they are. Richer structures (random slopes for time, or a correlation that falls off with distance) are possible, at the cost of more parameters.

Read the formula as “model asthma-control score using visit, arm and how their relationship changes, while allowing participants to have different starting levels.”

R notation How to read it
outcome ~ predictors The outcome on the left is modeled using the terms on the right
time_factor * studyarm Include visit, arm and the visit-by-arm interaction
(1 \| study_id_screening) Allow a participant-specific intercept
data = visits Find the named columns in this dataset

An interaction allows the arm difference to vary with visit. A factor tells R that values are categories. The reference category is the starting point against which the other categories are expressed.

Time as a factor, not a line

time_factor treats each visit (0, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12 months) as its own category, so the model estimates a separate mean for each arm at each visit without assuming a straight line or any other shape over time. With six visits that’s 12 means (6 visits × 2 arms). The price is more parameters than a linear trend; the gain is that a rise and then a fall, or an early change that levels off, are described as they are.

What the coefficients mean

With R’s default coding, control is the reference arm and baseline is the reference visit. The model time_factor * studyarm then has these fixed effects:

Coefficient Meaning
(Intercept) Mean in the control arm at baseline
studyarm1 Difference between arms at baseline
time_factor2, time_factor4, … Change from baseline in the control arm at each visit
time_factor2:studyarm1, … How much more (or less) the intervention arm changed from baseline than the control arm, at each visit

The last row matters most. Each visit-by-arm term is a difference in change from baseline: the arm difference at that visit minus the arm difference at baseline. This is what the plots mean by “adjusted for baseline differences”. This subtraction defines a change contrast; it does not guarantee that baseline imbalance or regression to the mean has been accounted for appropriately. It is different from using the baseline score as a covariate in an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) (Vickers and Altman 2001).

The arm difference at a given visit itself, with no baseline subtraction, is not one coefficient: it’s studyarm1 plus that visit’s interaction term. Adding a subgroup to this model is covered in Interactions. Getting such combinations, with their confidence intervals, is the subject of Effects behind an interaction.

A small invented example

These means are entirely invented, not trial results.

Arm Baseline score Follow-up score Change
Control 10 12 +2
Intervention 11 15 +4

The follow-up arm difference is 15 − 12 = 3 points. The difference in change is 4 − 2 = 2 points, equivalently the follow-up difference of 3 minus the baseline difference of 1. Both describe these numbers, but they answer different questions. Whether a score difference is clinically important requires the instrument’s interpretation, not just a p-value.

For a short explanation of baseline adjustment, see Vickers and Altman’s Statistics Note (Vickers and Altman 2001).

Missed visits and missing at random

A participant who misses a visit can still contribute their other observed measurements to a mixed model. This does not require filling in a score for the missed visit. Observations with missing model predictors, such as a subgroup classification, are a separate problem.

Missing at random (MAR) means that, after accounting for relevant information already recorded—such as treatment group and earlier asthma scores—whether someone misses a visit does not additionally depend on the asthma score we would have measured at that visit. “At random” does not mean that visits were missed without a reason (Sterne et al. 2009).

For example, missed visits related to previously recorded poor asthma control can be compatible with MAR. Missed visits related to unrecorded worsening symptoms may violate it, if that relationship remains after accounting for the observed information. These are hypothetical examples, not explanations established for this trial.

Likelihood-based mixed models can support valid inference under MAR when the outcome model is correctly specified and represents the relevant observed information. Choosing a mixed model does not establish MAR, and observed data alone cannot distinguish MAR from missingness that still depends on unobserved outcomes (Sterne et al. 2009).

What multiple imputation adds

Multiple imputation creates several plausible versions of the missing values, analyzes each completed dataset, and pools the estimates while accounting for uncertainty about the missing values. It does not recover the actual missed measurements (Sterne et al. 2009).

It is not automatically necessary just because outcome visits were missed: the mixed model already uses incomplete outcome trajectories under MAR. Imputation may add value when model predictors are missing or when other recorded variables help predict missingness and missing outcomes. For longitudinal analyses, the imputation model must respect repeated observations within participants and the interactions used in the analysis.

Ordinary MAR-based imputation does not solve missingness that depends on unobserved outcomes. Sensitivity analyses instead examine how conclusions change under explicitly different assumptions. Whether to add imputation or such analyses here remains a scientific decision; this explanation does not change the analysis.

For an accessible introduction, see Box 1 and the practical discussion in Sterne and colleagues’ BMJ article (Sterne et al. 2009).

Other models for the same question

The random-intercept mixed model is one of several reasonable ways to analyse repeated measures in a trial. Two common alternatives are worth knowing, because readers will meet them in other trials and because each makes a different trade-off. This trial uses neither; the comparison is here to explain the choice.

Linear mixed model (used here) Mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) Generalized estimating equations (GEE)
How it handles a person’s repeated visits A random intercept per participant No random effects; models the correlation between visits directly No random effects; a “working” guess at the correlation
Correlation between visits Implied: the same for any two visits Estimated freely for every pair of visits (unstructured) Treated as a nuisance; the standard errors are robust to a wrong guess
How it is fitted Likelihood Likelihood Estimating equations, with no full distributional assumption
Missed visits Valid if missing at random (see above) Valid if missing at random Valid only if missing completely at random, unless weighted
Main cost The equal-correlation assumption Many correlation parameters: 21 for six visits Weaker missing-data protection; robust standard errors are too small in small samples

MMRM is the most common primary analysis for continuous outcomes in regulatory trials with fixed visits (Mallinckrodt et al. 2008). Its unstructured correlation avoids assuming that a person’s visits two months apart are as alike as visits a year apart. The price is many parameters, which a small pilot may not support, especially within subgroups. In its usual form an MMRM also treats the baseline score as a covariate rather than as one of the outcomes, which changes the estimate from a difference in change from baseline to a baseline-adjusted difference at each visit (Coffman et al. 2016). That is a different question, not just a different method.

GEE describe the average outcome in each group without modelling individuals (Liang and Zeger 1986). Their robust standard errors stay valid even if the assumed correlation is wrong. But in their basic form they assume that missed visits are unrelated to anything measured, which is a stronger assumption than missing at random; weighting by the probability of attending each visit relaxes it (Robins et al. 1995). With few participants, robust standard errors are biased downwards and need a small-sample correction (Mancl and DeRouen 2001).

For a continuous outcome, all three can estimate the same treatment effect, and the same tools turn the fit into estimates and figures. This pilot uses the random-intercept model because it uses every observed visit under missing at random with few parameters.

Count outcomes are different

The 12-month health care use counts and the rescue inhaler counts are one value per participant, not repeated measures, so they don’t need a mixed model. They are fitted with negative binomial regression on the log scale, with the log of each person’s follow-up time as an offset, so the model describes rates (events per day of follow-up) rather than raw counts. On the log scale, exponentiated coefficients are incidence rate ratios: an arm effect of 0.8 means the intervention arm’s rate is 80% of the control arm’s.

Counts, rates and the offset

In an invented example, 6 events over 60 days and 3 events over 30 days have the same rate: 0.1 events per day. Comparing the counts alone would miss that equality. The offset supplies the exposure duration with its coefficient fixed, so the model compares rates. Negative binomial regression additionally allows counts to vary more than a Poisson model assumes. Neither choice repairs incorrectly defined event counts or follow-up windows.

An IRR of 1 means equal modeled rates; an IRR below 1 means a lower rate in the intervention arm, with the direction reversed above 1. A 95% confidence interval crossing 1 includes equal rates among the values compatible with the data and model at that confidence level; it does not prove equivalence.

For technical depth after the examples, Bates and colleagues explain the mixed-model implementation (Bates et al. 2015). The MASS reference documents the negative binomial function (MASS authors, n.d.).
